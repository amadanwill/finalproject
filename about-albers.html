Biography


Albers was born in the coal-mining city of Bottrop, Germany, in March 1888. In his early life, he taught first as a primary school teacher and later as an art teacher. From 1913, he also worked as a printmaker and began to practice with stained glass.

A year after its founding in 1920, Albers enrolled as a student at Das Staatliches Bauhaus. Known simply as the Bauhaus, the German art school aimed to break down barriers between fine and applied arts and to rebuild modern Germany after World War I. Albers studied there for three years in the school’s Vorkurs before he joined its staff as an instructor for stained glass. He would later go on to teach Werklehre, the principles of design.

Albers moved with Bauhaus from Weimar to Dessau in 1925, and was promoted to professor. That same year, he married Anni Fleischmann, a student at the institution.

Anni Albers, born Annelise Elsa Frieda Fleischmann, arrived at Bauhaus in 1922 after several years studying as a painter. Though she considered it “sissy” work, Fleischmann could only enroll at Bauhaus in the one workshop available to women: weaving. Despite her initial discontent, Fleischmann would grow to appreciate the medium and expand her knowledge of its artistic functions and applications. When her mentor left Bauhaus in 1931, she took over as head of the weaving workshop.

In 1933, the Nazi forced Bauhaus to close its doors due to its production of what the Third Reich considered “degenerate” art. Left without jobs and in fear for their safety, the Albers sought to emigrate and were given an opportunity through one of their associates, American architect Phillip Johnson.

Josef Albers joined the faculty of a then-new art school, Black Mountain College, as head of the painting program. Anni Albers joined as an assistant professor of art and later started a weaving studio. During this period, the two often traveled to Cuba and Mexico, the influence of which can be seen in their later work.

In 1950, the Alberses left Black Mountain, North Carolina, for New Haven, Connecticut, where Josef Albers headed Yale University’s design department. That year, he began the renowned Homage to the Square series, which explored color theory.

Albers retired from teaching in 1958, though he continued to paint. In 1963, Albers published the seminal Interaction of Color; in 1971, he established the research and educational nonprofit The Josef and Anni Albers Foundation; and in 1972, he became the first living artist to have a solo retrospective exhibition at the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

Josef Albers died in his sleep at Yale-New Haven Hospital on March 25, 1976, a week after his 88th birthday.

Anni Albers continued to design textiles and work alongside The Foundation. In 1983, she traveled to the opening of the Josef Albers Museum in Bottrop in 1983. On May 9, 1994, Anni Albers died in her home in Orange, Connecticut.
